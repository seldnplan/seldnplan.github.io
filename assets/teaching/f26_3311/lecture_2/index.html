Symmetry depends on structure

We return to the fifth roots of unity

\[\mu_5=\{1,\zeta_5,\zeta_5^2,\zeta_5^3,\zeta_5^4\},\qquad \zeta_5=e^{2\pi i/5}.\]
Guiding definition

A symmetry is a structure-preserving transformation. The same set can therefore have different collections of symmetries, depending on which structure we choose to preserve.

Today we place two different structures on $\mu_5$. First we remember that its elements multiply. Later we remember that they are the vertices of a regular pentagon in the plane.

The multiplicative structure

Multiplication of roots is controlled entirely by their exponents:

\[\zeta_5^i\zeta_5^j=\zeta_5^{i+j}.\]
Observation

Exponents are read modulo $5$: $\zeta_5^{i+5}=\zeta_5^i$. Thus we may regard the exponents as residue classes in $\mathbb Z/5\mathbb Z$.

Classifying the multiplicative symmetries

A multiplicative symmetry is a bijection $\sigma:\mu_5\to\mu_5$ satisfying

\[\sigma(\zeta_5^i)\sigma(\zeta_5^j)=\sigma(\zeta_5^{i+j})\qquad\text{for every }i,j.\]

Repeated multiplication then gives

\[\sigma(\zeta_5^k)=\sigma(\zeta_5)^k.\]

For example, $\sigma(\zeta_5^2)=\sigma(\zeta_5)^2$ and $\sigma(\zeta_5^3)=\sigma(\zeta_5)^3$. Consequently, the single value $\sigma(\zeta_5)$ determines every value of $\sigma$.

Why there are four

If $\sigma(\zeta_5)=1$, then $\sigma(\zeta_5^k)=1$ for every $k$, so $\sigma$ is not a bijection. Each of the other four choices is allowed. They give

\[\sigma_a(\zeta_5^k)=\zeta_5^{ak},\qquad a\in\{1,2,3,4\},\]

the identity, squaring, cubing, and fourth-power maps.

Composing multiplicative symmetries

Composition stays inside the same four-element collection. Indeed,

\[(\sigma_a\circ\sigma_b)(\zeta_5^k)=\zeta_5^{abk},\qquad\text{so}\qquad \sigma_a\circ\sigma_b=\sigma_{ab},\]

where the subscript is reduced modulo $5$.

Composition of the four multiplicative symmetries
$\circ$$\sigma_1$$\sigma_2$$\sigma_3$$\sigma_4$
$\sigma_1$$\sigma_1$$\sigma_2$$\sigma_3$$\sigma_4$
$\sigma_2$$\sigma_2$$\sigma_4$$\sigma_1$$\sigma_3$
$\sigma_3$$\sigma_3$$\sigma_1$$\sigma_4$$\sigma_2$
$\sigma_4$$\sigma_4$$\sigma_3$$\sigma_2$$\sigma_1$

If we keep only the subscripts, this is exactly multiplication of the nonzero residue classes modulo $5$:

Multiplication in $(\mathbb Z/5\mathbb Z)^\times$
$\cdot$$1$$2$$3$$4$
$1$$1$$2$$3$$4$
$2$$2$$4$$1$$3$
$3$$3$$1$$4$$2$
$4$$4$$3$$2$$1$

The table is symmetric across its diagonal, so these four symmetries commute with one another.

Rigid symmetries of the pentagon

Now forget multiplication and view $\mu_5$ as the vertices of a regular pentagon. A rigid symmetry is a transformation of the plane that carries this pentagon onto itself.

Generators

Let $r$ be counterclockwise rotation through $2\pi/5$, and let $s$ be reflection across the real axis. In the handwritten lecture notes these were denoted $\sigma$ and $\tau$, respectively.

Reflections

The reflection $s$ is complex conjugation. It fixes $1$ and exchanges the remaining roots in pairs. Rotating its axis produces four more reflections.

Rotations and order of composition

The rotation $r$ moves each vertex one step counterclockwise:

\[1\longmapsto\zeta_5\longmapsto\zeta_5^2\longmapsto\zeta_5^3\longmapsto\zeta_5^4\longmapsto1.\]

Unlike the multiplicative symmetries, rigid symmetries need not commute. Reading composition from right to left,

\[(r\circ s)(1)=\zeta_5,\qquad (s\circ r)(1)=\zeta_5^4,\]

so $r\circ s\ne s\circ r$. This is the first example of noncommutativity.

All rigid symmetries

The five rotations $e,r,r^2,r^3,r^4$ and the five reflections $s,rs,r^2s,r^3s,r^4s$ give every rigid symmetry of the pentagon. Together they form the dihedral group $D_{10}$ of order $10$, with relations

\[r^5=e,\qquad s^2=e,\qquad srs=r^{-1}.\]

Abstracting the pattern: groups

Both collections of symmetries are closed under composition, contain a do-nothing symmetry, and allow every motion to be undone. This common pattern is the definition of a group.

Definition

A group is a triple $(G,*,e)$ consisting of a set $G$, a binary operation $*:G\times G\to G$, and a distinguished element $e\in G$, such that:

  1. Identity: $e*g=g=g*e$ for every $g\in G$.
  2. Associativity: $g_1*(g_2*g_3)=(g_1*g_2)*g_3$ for all $g_1,g_2,g_3\in G$.
  3. Inverses: for every $g\in G$, there is an element $h\in G$ such that $h*g=e$.
Motivation for associativity

For functions $f_1,f_2,f_3$ and every input $x$,

\[(f_1\circ(f_2\circ f_3))(x)=f_1(f_2(f_3(x)))=((f_1\circ f_2)\circ f_3)(x).\]

Thus composition automatically supplies the associativity required in the group definition. This is the primary motivation for the condition above: Most groups we encounter will be structure-preserving symmetries of some object with the operation being composition, so associativity is automatic in these cases.

The four multiplication-preserving symmetries form a commutative group isomorphic to $(\mathbb Z/5\mathbb Z)^\times$, a notion we will consider next week. The ten rigid symmetries form the noncommutative group $D_{10}$, also knowns as the dihedral group of order 10.

You'll verify the following lemma in the next homework. The main point behind it is that inverses are unique and are also inverses on the right. In other words, double-inverse gets you back where you started. We could have made these properties part of the axioms, but it turns out we don't have to. The seemingly weaker condition imposed in the definition implies something actually stronger

Lemma: inverses are unique

Given a group $(G,*,e)$ and an element $g\in G$, there is a unique element $g^{-1}\in G$ such that $g^{-1}*g = e$. Moreover, we also have $g*g^{-1}=e$, so that $(g^{-1})^{-1} = g$
