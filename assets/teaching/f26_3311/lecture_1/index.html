What is algebra?

In simple terms, algebra is what turns up when we notice that ostensibly different kinds of mathematical objects have the same essential structures governing their behavior. We extract that essential structure and formalize it as axioms, which let us reason abstractly and develop results that apply to every object with the same structure.

In this course, we will study examples of such essential structures, including:

  • Groups
  • Rings
  • Fields

Roots, choices, and symmetry

Example 1

Step back for a moment to high-school algebra and consider

\[x^2-2=0.\]

It has two solutions, $\pm\sqrt{2}$. How do we tell them apart? One is positive and the other is negative. The positive root is also the only one of the two that has a real square root, namely $\sqrt[4]{2}$.

Example 2

Now consider

\[x^2+1=0.\]

It also has two solutions, $\pm i$. Unlike the previous example, there is no God-given choice between them. All we can say intrinsically is that there are two roots and that each is the negative of the other.

Example 3

The zeros of $x^2+x+1$ are

\[\frac{-1\pm i\sqrt{3}}{2}.\]

The sign $\pm$ accounts for the ambiguity in choosing $i$: either choice produces the same pair of complex numbers, although it does not distinguish one root from the other.

The reason is a symmetry: complex conjugation, $a+ib\mapsto a-ib$, exchanges the two roots. More generally, every quadratic polynomial with real coefficients and no real roots has two roots related by complex conjugation, with no systematic way to break the symmetry between them.

From Abel to Galois

A forward-looking remark

The quadratic formula was known, but for centuries mathematicians sought analogous formulas for higher-degree equations. Italian mathematicians settled the cubic and quartic cases in the sixteenth century. In the early nineteenth century, Niels Henrik Abel proved that no formula by radicals exists for the general quintic.

Soon afterward, Évariste Galois—who died in a duel at the age of twenty-one—explained this phenomenon by studying the symmetries of polynomial roots. His work led directly to group theory, our first focus this semester. We will return to Galois's ideas near the end of this semester and study them much more closely next semester.

Fifth roots of unity

Consider the equation $x^5-1=0$. Its solutions are the five fifth roots of unity:

\[1,\ \zeta_5,\ \zeta_5^2,\ \zeta_5^3,\ \zeta_5^4,\qquad \zeta_5=e^{2\pi i/5}.\]

A complex number of length $1$ at angle $\theta$ to the real axis is $e^{i\theta}$. Multiplication adds angles, so

\[(e^{i\theta})^n=e^{in\theta},\qquad e^{2m\pi i}=1\quad(m,n\in\mathbb Z).\]

Consequently, $\zeta_5^5=e^{2\pi i}=1$, and the same is true for every power of $\zeta_5$. More generally, if $\zeta_n=e^{2\pi i/n}$, then every power of $\zeta_n$ is a solution of $x^n-1=0$. Geometrically, the fifth roots lie at the vertices of a regular pentagon on the unit circle.

Symmetries by taking powers

We can distinguish $1$ from the other fifth roots because it is the only real root. But the other four cannot be distinguished naturally. We could name any one of them $\zeta_5$, and the non-real roots would still be $\zeta_5,\zeta_5^2,\zeta_5^3,\zeta_5^4$.

Complex conjugation supplies one symmetry: it exchanges $\zeta_5$ with $\zeta_5^4$ and $\zeta_5^2$ with $\zeta_5^3$. Cubing gives another:

\[\zeta_5\longmapsto\zeta_5^3\longmapsto\zeta_5^9=\zeta_5^4 \longmapsto\zeta_5^{12}=\zeta_5^2\longmapsto\zeta_5^6=\zeta_5.\]

The simplifications use $\zeta_5^5=1$. In general, $\zeta_5^m$ depends only on the remainder of $m$ after division by $5$, because

\[\zeta_5^{5q+r}=(\zeta_5^5)^q\zeta_5^r=\zeta_5^r.\]

Thus the symmetries of the nontrivial fifth roots move every one of them to every other one; no distinguished proper subset survives.

The twelfth roots of unity

The twelfth roots break into pieces with different algebraic features:

  1. $1$: the most distinguished root, and the root of $x-1=0$.
  2. $-1$: it squares to $1$ and is the root of $x+1=0$.
  3. Primitive cube roots: $\zeta_{12}^4$ and $\zeta_{12}^8$, the zeros of $x^2+x+1$.
  4. Primitive fourth roots: $i=\zeta_{12}^3$ and $-i=\zeta_{12}^9$, the zeros of $x^2+1$.
  5. Primitive sixth roots: $\zeta_{12}^2$ and $\zeta_{12}^{10}$, which cube to $-1$ and are the zeros of
    \[x^2-x+1=\frac{x^3+1}{x+1}.\]
  6. Primitive twelfth roots: $\zeta_{12},\zeta_{12}^5,\zeta_{12}^7,\zeta_{12}^{11}$. No positive power smaller than $12$ sends any of them to $1$; they are the zeros of $x^4-x^2+1$.

Bézout's identity

Given integers $a,b\in\mathbb Z$, there are integers $s,t\in\mathbb Z$ such that

\[\gcd(a,b)=sa+tb.\]

In particular, the following are equivalent:

  1. $a$ and $b$ are relatively prime.
  2. $1$ is an integer linear combination of $a$ and $b$.
Congruence modulo $n$

Two integers are equivalent modulo $n$ when their difference is divisible by $n$—equivalently, when they leave the same remainder after division by $n$. We write

\[a\equiv b\pmod n.\]

The equivalence classes form $\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z$. Addition and multiplication of classes can be pictured as arithmetic on a clock.
